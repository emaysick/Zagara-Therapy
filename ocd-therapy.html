OCD can take up more room than you want it to.

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) can involve unwanted, intrusive thoughts, images, urges, or doubts that bring distress, along with actions or mental routines used to try to reduce that distress or feel certain. These responses can bring temporary relief, but often reinforce the cycle over time, leaving you feeling pulled back into the same questions, fears, or rituals.

OCD looks different from person to person. Some compulsions are visible; others happen almost entirely in the mind.

OCD can show up in many ways

  • Checking, repeating, or reviewing events and conversations
  • Seeking reassurance or researching to feel certain
  • Intrusive thoughts or images about harm, relationships, morality, identity, or making a mistake
  • Contamination fears or concerns about illness and bodily sensations
  • Mental rituals, counting, comparing, or trying to find the “right” feeling
  • A strong need for things to feel complete, correct, or just right

This list isn't a diagnostic checklist. If you recognize some of these patterns, a consultation can help us explore what you're experiencing and whether OCD-focused treatment may be appropriate.

What is Exposure and Response Prevention?

Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) is a first-line, evidence-based treatment for OCD. It involves gradually and collaboratively approaching situations, thoughts, sensations, or uncertainties that trigger distress while practicing a different response instead of engaging in compulsions, avoidance, or reassurance seeking.

ERP isn't about overwhelming you, forcing you into situations before you're ready, or proving that feared outcomes are impossible. It's about building the ability to experience uncertainty and discomfort without feeling compelled to resolve, escape, or neutralize them. Over time, this can help OCD have less influence over your choices and make more room for the life you want to live.

What treatment can look like

  • Understanding your OCD cycle and the ways compulsions may be keeping it going
  • Building a treatment plan around your goals, circumstances, and readiness to practice
  • Collaboratively developing ERP exercises and practicing response prevention
  • Making space for uncertainty without relying on reassurance or perfect certainty
  • Reviewing progress and adjusting treatment as we learn what is useful

Treatment is collaborative and individualized. My work with OCD is informed by specialized training through the International OCD Foundation's Behavior Therapy Training Institute (BTTI). We can talk about questions, concerns, and what makes exposures feel difficult rather than treating therapy as a test you have to pass.

A note about treatment

OCD treatment is not about eliminating every intrusive thought or reaching absolute certainty. It focuses on changing the patterns that give OCD more influence over your choices, so you can make room for uncertainty and move toward what matters.
